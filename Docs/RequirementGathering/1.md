# Insurance domain to application developers 

### **From the business problem first, to software components**

The important message for developers is: **Before you build an Insurance Application, understand what an Insurance Company actually does.**
>
> Code is only the implementation. **Domain knowledge tells you what to build.**

## 🏦 Insurance Domain — A Developer's Learning Story

Let us imagine that our customer is **LIC — Life Insurance Corporation of India**. LIC wants us to build an **Insurance Management Platform** that will be used by three major groups:

1. **LIC Staff / Employees**
2. **LIC Agents**
3. **LIC Customers / Policyholders**

Our job as application developers is not simply to create screens and APIs. We need to understand the **business journey**.

## 1. Start With the Real-World Story

Imagine **Ravi**, a customer, walks into an LIC office. He says: "I want to buy life insurance." The LIC employee or agent doesn't immediately create a database record. There is a business process.

### Customer → Agent → Proposal → Underwriting → Policy → Premium → Claim

This single sentence can become the foundation of our entire application. As developers, we should ask:

**What are the nouns?**

* Customer
* Agent
* Employee
* Product
* Proposal
* Policy
* Premium
* Payment
* Nominee
* Claim
* Beneficiary

These nouns gradually become our **domain entities**. Then ask:

**What are the actions?**

* Register customer
* Find insurance product
* Create proposal
* Submit proposal
* Approve proposal
* Issue policy
* Pay premium
* Renew policy
* Change nominee
* Raise claim
* Verify claim
* Approve claim
* Reject claim
* Settle claim

These actions gradually become our **use cases / services / APIs**.
 

## 2. Understand the Three Actors

Our application has three primary users.

### 👤 Customer

The customer wants to:

* Register
* Browse insurance products
* Buy a policy
* Submit proposal
* Pay premium
* View policy
* Download documents
* Update profile
* Add/change nominee
* Check premium schedule
* Submit claim
* Track claim status

For the customer:

> **Insurance Application = My policies + My payments + My claims**

 

### 👨‍💼 LIC Agent

An agent is a very important actor.

The agent acts as a bridge between LIC and customers.

An agent may:

* Register customers
* Recommend insurance products
* Create proposals
* Submit proposals
* Track proposal status
* Track policies
* Help customers pay premiums
* Assist with claims
* Track commissions
* Manage assigned customers

So for an agent:

> **Insurance Application = My customers + My proposals + My policies + My commissions**

This immediately changes our application design. A customer sees **his policies**. An agent sees **his customers and their policies**.

 
### 👨‍💼 LIC Staff

Now consider an LIC employee. Different employees have different responsibilities. For example:

##### Policy Officer

Can:

* Review proposal
* Verify documents
* Approve policy
* Reject proposal
* Modify policy

##### Claims Officer

Can:

* View claims
* Verify documents
* Investigate claim
* Approve/reject claim
* Initiate settlement

##### Accounts Officer

Can:

* Verify payments
* Process refunds
* Process commissions
* Process claim settlements

##### Manager

Can:

* Monitor employees
* Monitor agents
* View business reports
* Approve exceptional cases

Therefore: **One application does not mean one permission model.** We need **Role-Based Access Control — RBAC**.

 
## 5. The Most Important Insurance Concept: Policy

Now we reach the heart of the domain. 

### What is a Policy?

A policy is essentially a **contract between the insurance company and the policyholder**.

For example:

```text
Policy Number: LIC10001234

Customer: Ravi
Product: Life Protection Plan

Sum Assured: ₹50,00,000
Premium: ₹25,000/year

Policy Term: 20 years

Start Date: 01-Jan-2026
End Date: 31-Dec-2045

Status: Active
```

As developers, we might model:

```text
Policy
 ├── PolicyNumber
 ├── Customer
 ├── Product
 ├── SumAssured
 ├── Premium
 ├── StartDate
 ├── EndDate
 └── Status
```

But **don't stop here**. A good developer asks:  "What happens to this policy during its lifetime?" That takes us to the **Policy Lifecycle**.
 

## 6. Policy Lifecycle

A policy doesn't simply exist. It moves through states.

```text
Proposal
   ↓
Submitted
   ↓
Under Review
   ↓
Approved
   ↓
Policy Issued
   ↓
Active
   ↓
Premium Due
   ↓
Premium Paid
   ↓
Renewed
   ↓
Matured
```

There can also be:

```text
Active
   ↓
Lapsed
   ↓
Revived
   ↓
Active
```

or

```text
Active
   ↓
Cancelled
```

or

```text
Active
   ↓
Claim
   ↓
Settled
```

This is where developers start discovering something important: **Business domains are state machines.** That knowledge directly influences:

* Database design
* API design
* Validation
* UI
* Workflow
* Notifications
* Audit
* Testing
 

## 7. Premium — The Money Flow

The customer doesn't simply "have a policy." The customer has an obligation to pay premiums.

For example:

```text
Policy Premium = ₹25,000/year
```

Payment schedule:

```text
01-Jan-2026 → ₹25,000
01-Jan-2027 → ₹25,000
01-Jan-2028 → ₹25,000
...
```

So we may need:

```text
Policy
   │
   └── PremiumSchedule
           │
           ├── PremiumDue
           ├── DueDate
           ├── PaidDate
           ├── Amount
           └── Status
```

Now application developers need to think about:

* Payment gateway
* Payment confirmation
* Failed payments
* Receipts
* Late payments
* Reminders
* Auto-renewal
* Reconciliation

This is where **business domain meets distributed systems**.

 

## 8. Claim — Another Major Business Process

Suppose Ravi has a life insurance policy. Unfortunately, a claim event occurs. The nominee submits a claim. The system now starts another workflow:

```text
Claim Raised
     ↓
Documents Submitted
     ↓
Claim Verification
     ↓
Investigation
     ↓
Claim Assessment
     ↓
Approved / Rejected
     ↓
Settlement
```

A developer might initially think:  "Claim is just another CRUD table." No. **Claim is a business workflow.** It may involve:

* Customer
* Policy
* Nominee
* Documents
* Claims Officer
* Investigation
* Approval
* Finance
* Payment
* Audit

That is why domain understanding matters.

For our LIC application, I would initially teach students this model:

```text

                           ↓
                    ┌─────────────┐
                    │   Policy    │
                    └──────┬──────┘
                           │
             ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
             ↓             ↓             ↓
        ┌─────────┐   ┌──────────┐   ┌─────────┐

```

This becomes our **domain map**.
 

## 10. Proposal — Don't Forget This

One of the common mistakes beginners make is: 

```text
Customer → Policy
```

In reality, there is usually an important stage before policy issuance.

```text
Customer
   ↓
Insurance Product
   ↓
Proposal
   ↓
Underwriting
   ↓
Approval
   ↓
Policy
```

The **proposal** contains information needed to evaluate the risk. For example:

```text
Customer Details
Personal Details
Age
Occupation
Income
Medical Information
Insurance Amount
Term
Nominee
Documents
```

The insurance company evaluates this information before issuing the policy. That evaluation is called **underwriting**.

## 11. Insurance Product vs Policy

Another important distinction for developers.

### Product

What LIC offers.

Example:

```text
Life Protection Plan
Child Plan
Pension Plan
Savings Plan
```

### Policy

A customer's actual contract based on a product.

So:

```text
Product
   │
   ├── Policy 1001
   ├── Policy 1002
   ├── Policy 1003
   └── Policy 1004
```

Therefore: **Product is a template/business offering. Policy is a customer's instance of that offering.** This is a very important object-oriented modeling concept.

## 14. Then Introduce AI

Only after students understand the **traditional Insurance Application**, I would introduce AI. Because: **AI cannot compensate for poor domain understanding.** Now imagine a customer asks: **"What documents are required to submit a death claim?"** This is a **knowledge question**.

Our AI system can use:

```text
Customer
   ↓
AI Assistant
   ↓
LLM
   ↓
RAG
   ↓
Vector Database
   ↓
LIC Policies / Manuals / Claim Rules
```

But suppose the customer asks: **"What is the status of my claim #CLM10045?"** That's not primarily a RAG question. The system needs to access the actual claim system:

```text
Customer
   ↓
AI Agent
   ↓
Tool Calling
   ↓
Claim API
   ↓
Database
   ↓
Claim Status
```

This gives students a beautiful distinction:

### RAG

> **"What does LIC's claim policy say?"**

### Tool Calling

> **"What is the status of MY claim?"**

### LLM

> Understands the question and generates the response.

### Vector Database

> Helps retrieve relevant insurance knowledge.

### MCP / Tools

> Gives the AI controlled access to business capabilities.

## The One Lesson I Want Every Developer to Remember

When LIC gives you a requirement: **"Build a Claim Management Module."** Don't immediately open Visual Studio and create:

```text
ClaimController.cs
ClaimService.cs
ClaimRepository.cs
Claim.cs
```

First ask: **What is a claim?** Then:

- Who creates it?
- Against what?
- What documents are required?
- Who verifies it?
- What states can it have?
- Who can approve it?
- What happens when it is rejected?
- Who performs the settlement?
- What financial transaction happens?
- What notifications are generated?
- What should be audited?
- Which parts require real-time data?
- Which parts require knowledge retrieval?
- Which parts can AI assist with?

**Now you are thinking like an application engineer—not just a programmer.**

And that is the core of the **Transflower Mentor approach**:
- 🌱 **First understand the business.**
- 🌱 **Then understand the problem.**
- 🌱 **Then model the domain.**
- 🌱 **Then design the architecture.**
- 🌱 **Then write the code.**
- 🌱 **Finally, use AI to make the application smarter.**
Let us take one fictional LIC requirement at a time and convert it into **Use Case → Domain Model → DB Schema → REST API → ASP.NET Core implementation → Angular/React UI → AI/RAG/Agent capability**.
